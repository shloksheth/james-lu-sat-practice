# Lesson 16 · Punctuation practice pack

34 flashcards · 32 original questions · full answer-choice explanations

## How to use this pack

1. Answer cards aloud before revealing the back. Give the rule and an example; recognizing the wording is not enough.
2. Complete questions 1–10 to establish the basics, then 11–20 for application, then 21–32 for mixed challenges. Write the sentence skeleton and your rule before checking the answer.
3. For a first attempt, prioritize accuracy. On a later attempt, use 24 minutes for all 32 questions as a training target, not an official SAT timing rule.
4. Keep an error log: question number, your answer, actual rule, why your choice failed, and a corrected example. Review missed cards tomorrow, in three days, and in a week.
5. After reviewing an error, solve the matched transfer item without notes. Suggested pairs: 1→30; 2→25; 4→31; 5→15; 7→28; 8→26; 9→32; 10→18; 11→29; 16→22/23.

## A dependable solving routine

Inspect the choices. Check for an integrated title/name, an existing dash pair, a direct/indirect question, or a complex list. Identify the main subject and finite verb; bracket true insertions. Classify both sides of the blank. Use CPIN as a checking order: colon; period/semicolon/comma + FANBOYS; isolated comma; no punctuation. Prove the chosen punctuation fits. If a transition can sit on either side of a boundary, read the surrounding statements and identify where the contrast or other relationship occurs.

## Guardrails for the shortcuts

- An -ing word does not by itself make a complete clause; it can also be part of a finite verb such as “is running.”
- A colon needs a complete clause on the left and a genuine explanatory relationship. Do not split a verb or preposition from its object.
- Removing a phrase must preserve the intended identification, not just leave a grammatical sentence. Restrictive clauses can start with words other than “that.”
- An identifying profession + name stays together; a supplementary name after a uniquely identified role can take paired commas.
- A following because-clause usually stays integrated without a comma. Introductory dependent clauses generally take a comma at their end.
- Periods and semicolons can serve similar clause-boundary functions, but capitalization and complex lists can distinguish the actual choices.
- Clausal contrastive “however” is different from “however difficult the task was.” A word’s grammatical role matters more than its spelling.
- The checking order is a memory aid. Reported answer-choice frequencies are not evidence that a particular answer is correct.

## Flashcards

### Card 01 · Method

**Front:** What should you inspect first in a punctuation question?

**Back:** Inspect the choices to identify the punctuation being tested. Then examine the relevant sentence structure. A candidate is correct only if it fits the whole sentence.

### Card 02 · Method

**Front:** What does CPIN stand for in the lesson?

**Back:** Colon; Period/semicolon/comma + FANBOYS; Isolated comma; No punctuation. Use it as a checking order, never as proof that a higher-priority choice is correct.

### Card 03 · Clauses

**Front:** What makes an independent clause?

**Back:** A subject and a finite verb expressing a complete thought: “The antenna receives signals.” A finite verb carries tense or follows a modal; an -ing word alone is not enough.

### Card 04 · Clauses

**Front:** Is “although the antenna receives signals” an independent clause? Why?

**Back:** No. Although makes it dependent. “The antenna receives signals” is independent; adding a subordinating word changes its status.

### Card 05 · Clauses

**Front:** Is “the antenna receiving signals from three satellites” a complete sentence?

**Back:** No. Receiving is a participle here. There is no finite main verb. “The antenna is receiving signals” is complete.

### Card 06 · Clauses

**Front:** How do you find the sentence skeleton when a subject is followed by extra information?

**Back:** Temporarily bracket a genuine nonessential insertion, then connect the subject to its main verb. “The antenna, installed last spring, receives signals” → “The antenna receives signals.”

### Card 07 · Boundaries

**Front:** Can a comma alone connect two independent clauses?

**Back:** No. That produces a comma splice. Use an appropriate period, semicolon, or comma plus coordinating conjunction; a colon may work when the second part explains the first.

### Card 08 · Boundaries

**Front:** When does a semicolon join clauses correctly?

**Back:** Both sides must be independent clauses. “The antenna failed; the team replaced it.” A fragment after the semicolon makes this use invalid.

### Card 09 · Boundaries

**Front:** What are the two main semicolon jobs to check?

**Back:** Joining independent clauses and separating complex list items. In a complex list, the items do not need to be complete clauses.

### Card 10 · Boundaries

**Front:** What does FANBOYS stand for?

**Back:** For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. When one joins two independent clauses, a comma usually comes before it. Check that the second part really has its own subject and verb.

### Card 11 · Boundaries

**Front:** Does “and” automatically require a comma before it?

**Back:** No. “The team calibrated the sensor and recorded the results” has a shared subject and compound predicate. Do not insert a clause-separating comma merely because and appears.

### Card 12 · Colon

**Front:** What must come before a colon used to introduce an explanation or list?

**Back:** A complete independent clause. “The team needed one tool: a wrench.” The material after the colon explains or specifies the statement before it.

### Card 13 · Colon

**Front:** Must the material after a colon be an independent clause?

**Back:** No. It may be a word, phrase, list, or complete clause. “The team had one concern: overheating.” The left side must still be complete.

### Card 14 · Colon

**Front:** Why is “The required tools include: a wrench and a drill” incorrect?

**Back:** The colon separates include from its direct objects. “The required tools include a wrench and a drill” is correct. Alternatively: “Two tools are required: a wrench and a drill.”

### Card 15 · Colon

**Front:** Why is a colon not automatically correct between two complete clauses?

**Back:** The second part must explain, identify, exemplify, or expand the first. Mere completeness on both sides does not establish that relationship.

### Card 16 · Dashes

**Front:** How do you check a dash that closes an interruption?

**Back:** Locate the opening dash and identify the entire insertion. Use a matching closing dash when the main sentence resumes. Remove the insertion to verify the skeleton.

### Card 17 · Dashes

**Front:** Can a single dash introduce an explanation?

**Back:** Yes: “The team needed one tool—a wrench.” Do not assume every dash is one half of a pair; determine whether it introduces an ending explanation or encloses an insertion.

### Card 18 · Insertions

**Front:** How do you punctuate a nonessential phrase in the middle of a sentence?

**Back:** Usually with paired commas, paired dashes, or parentheses. Both boundaries matter. “The mural, restored last year, attracts visitors.” Do not mix an opening comma with a closing dash.

### Card 19 · Insertions

**Front:** Is “I can remove it and the sentence still works” enough to prove a phrase is nonessential?

**Back:** No. Removal must also preserve the intended identification and meaning. “The paintings that survived the fire were restored” limits which paintings; deleting the relative clause changes that claim.

### Card 20 · Insertions

**Front:** How are restrictive relative clauses usually punctuated?

**Back:** No surrounding commas: “The samples that remained dry were usable.” The clause identifies a subset. Restrictiveness is about meaning; the pronoun alone is not a complete test.

### Card 21 · Insertions

**Front:** What is wrong with “The researcher, published the results”?

**Back:** The comma splits the subject from its main verb. A real insertion can occur between them, but it must be enclosed: “The researcher, after checking the data, published the results.”

### Card 22 · Insertions

**Front:** When is a comma appropriate after a dependent clause?

**Back:** A dependent clause that introduces the main clause generally takes a comma: “Although the device was old, it worked.” A following integrated clause usually does not: “It worked because the team repaired it.”

### Card 23 · Lists

**Front:** How do you punctuate a simple three-item list?

**Back:** Use commas between items: “The kit contains tape, string, and scissors.” On SAT-style questions, inspect the whole list before deciding what the blank separates.

### Card 24 · Lists

**Front:** Why do some lists use semicolons?

**Back:** Semicolons make item boundaries clear when items contain internal commas: “Reno, Nevada; Albany, New York; and Boise, Idaho.” Keep each entire item together.

### Card 25 · Lists

**Front:** How do you locate the semicolon in “Ada, a chemist; Leo, a physicist; and Mei, an engineer”?

**Back:** Each item is a person plus a description. The commas connect names to descriptions; the semicolons separate complete items. Match the repeated item structure.

### Card 26 · Names

**Front:** When does a profession directly before a name take no punctuation?

**Back:** When the profession and name identify a single person as one integrated unit: “Biologist Mara Chen spoke.” No comma goes between biologist and Mara Chen.

### Card 27 · Names

**Front:** Why can “Our only guide, Mara Chen, spoke” take commas?

**Back:** Our only guide already identifies a unique person in the stated context. Mara Chen supplies supplementary naming information. This is different from an integrated profession + name.

### Card 28 · Questions

**Front:** How do you distinguish a direct question from an indirect question?

**Back:** “Where did the team work?” directly asks and takes a question mark. “The report explains where the team worked.” makes a statement and takes a period. Judge the entire sentence.

### Card 29 · Transitions

**Front:** Can “however” connect independent clauses with commas alone?

**Back:** No. Contrastive however is not a coordinating conjunction. Use “The model was simple; however, it was accurate” or “The model was simple. However, it was accurate.”

### Card 30 · Transitions

**Front:** How does punctuation change which clause “however” belongs to?

**Back:** “The model was simple, however; it was accurate” attaches however to the first clause. “The model was simple; however, it was accurate” attaches it to the second. Read the preceding sentence to locate the contrast.

### Card 31 · Transitions

**Front:** How can you tell whether “though” is a dependent-clause starter or a parenthetical transition?

**Back:** “Though the model was simple, it was accurate” begins with a dependent clause. “The model was simple, though; it was accurate” uses parenthetical though. Determine its grammatical role.

### Card 32 · Accuracy

**Front:** If a period and a semicolon are both offered, can you always eliminate both?

**Back:** No. A semicolon may separate list items, a period may require a capital letter, and the choices may include other differing words. Substitute the complete choices and check the actual structure.

### Card 33 · Accuracy

**Front:** If two punctuation choices both seem grammatical, what should you check next?

**Back:** Check capitalization, list boundaries, whether an insertion closes, and which clause a transition belongs to. Do not invent a stylistic preference to break a true tie.

### Card 34 · Accuracy

**Front:** What should you say to yourself before submitting a boundary answer?

**Back:** “Left side: complete or incomplete? Right side: complete clause, explanation, insertion, or list item? What exact rule permits my choice?” Name the structural reason rather than relying on a pause.

**Structure:** [marsh, which flooded]; [meadow beside the reservoir]; and [woodland, which remained shaded].

- **A:** A period breaks the list and leaves the and the woodland continuation as a fragment.
- **B:** A comma fails to preserve the established semicolon item boundaries.
- **D:** A colon falsely makes the woodland an explanation of the meadow.

### 30 · B · Accuracy

**Why it works:** Both sides are independent clauses. A semicolon works with lowercase digital; the period choice leaves the new sentence uncapitalized.

**Structure:** A program makes the collection available / digital reproductions can be viewed online.

- **A:** After a period, Digital would need a capital D.
- **C:** A comma alone creates a comma splice.
- **D:** No punctuation creates a run-on.

### 31 · C · Colon

**Why it works:** The complete left clause announces an implication, and the following complete clause explains exactly what that implication is.

**Structure:** The carpenter’s assessment had one clear implication / both the frame and the door needed replacement.

- **A:** That makes the right side dependent, so it cannot follow a clause-joining semicolon.
- **B:** The comma alone joins two independent clauses and creates a comma splice.
- **D:** No punctuation creates a run-on between the independent clauses.

### 32 · A · Names

**Why it works:** Historian Nia Okafor is integrated, while the supplementary description following the name needs paired commas.

**Structure:** Historian Nia Okafor, [a specialist in regional trade], described the trade routes.

- **B:** The supplementary noun phrase is not enclosed.
- **C:** The semicolon splits the subject from its main verb and begins a fragment.
- **D:** The description has an opening comma but no closing comma before described.
