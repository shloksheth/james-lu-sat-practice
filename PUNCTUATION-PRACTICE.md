# Answers and explanations

1. **C — Noun-clause subjects**
The entire that-clause is the subject; surprised is the main finite verb. In A, whether introduces an embedded question without an outer predicate. B lacks a finite predicate. Although makes D dependent.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=67s)

2. **C — Gerund subjects and embedded verbs**
Subject: Testing whether the two coatings resist saltwater. Main verb: requires. Resist belongs to the embedded whether-clause; simulates belongs to the relative clause modifying equipment. Testing is a gerund in the subject.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=336s)

3. **B — Auxiliary plus -ing**
Are comparing is a finite verb phrase: the auxiliary are supplies tense. An -ing word alone is not a finite predicate, but it can be part of one. A, C, and D lack an outer finite predicate.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=128s)

4. **C — Dependent-clause starters**
Although makes C dependent. A has main verb detected, B has main verb was, and D has the finite verb phrase was detecting. The initial word alone does not determine whether the entire construction is complete.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=612s)

5. **A — Inverted clause structure**
This is an inverted clause: stands is the finite verb, and a weather-monitoring station is the subject that follows it. A subject need not appear first. The other choices do not supply a complete main clause.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=67s)

6. **B — Nested relative clauses**
Strip away the embedded relative clauses: The method reduces the time. Surveyed and developed are verbs inside modifiers; neither is the outer predicate of method.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=106s)

7. **B — Colon introducing a list**
The restoration team chose three pigments is a complete clause. The following noun list names those pigments, so a colon introduces the explanation. A period or semicolon would leave the list as a sentence fragment. Comma plus which would begin a relative clause that has no predicate.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=770s)

8. **D — Verb–object boundary**
Included directly takes the following list as its object. The words before the blank are incomplete without that object; do not insert a colon, dash, or semicolon between them.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=919s)

9. **B — Two independent clauses**
Clause 1: The old clock no longer keeps accurate time. Clause 2: its engraved face remains unusually well preserved. A semicolon joins them. A comma alone is a splice; no punctuation is a run-on. And followed by a semicolon breaks the coordinating conjunction from the clause it should introduce.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=961s)

10. **C — Period before an introductory modifier**
The first clause ends with deep water. The second starts with a participial modifier, developed by a team of marine engineers, and then supplies the complete clause it can withstand substantial pressure. The opening modifier does not make the second sentence a fragment.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=5119s)

11. **A — Comma plus coordinating conjunction**
Researchers sealed and a technician recorded are separate subject–verb pairs. Use a comma plus and to join the independent clauses. The other choices either create an inappropriate boundary before and or omit the needed clause separator.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1368s)

12. **D — Shared-subject compound predicate**
One subject, technician, performs both actions: recorded and placed. Placed the containers does not have its own subject. Keep the compound predicate together without a punctuation boundary after and.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1368s)

13. **B — Introductory dependent clause**
Although makes the first clause dependent. A comma connects that introductory dependent clause to the main clause, the material beneath it remained structurally sound. A period or semicolon would strand the dependent clause.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1685s)

14. **C — Long subject before its main verb**
The whether-clause is the complete subject of remains. Do not separate a subject from its main verb with punctuation. This is a full sentence even though its subject contains an embedded question.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=457s)

15. **D — Closing a comma pair**
A collection of handwritten field notes is a nonessential appositive already opened by a comma. A second comma closes it. The underlying clause is The archive’s newest acquisition documents changes.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1768s)

16. **A — Opening a comma pair**
Which was installed more than a century ago adds supplemental information about an already identified telescope. The comma after ago closes that modifier; a comma at the blank opens it. The main clause is The telescope still produces images.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2299s)

17. **C — Matching paired dashes**
The opening dash before released needs a matching closing dash. Remove the interruption: The software update reduced the time. A comma cannot close a dash pair.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2359s)

18. **B — Correct extent of a dash interruption**
The blank comes immediately before the existing word which. Insert a dash there so the entire which-clause lies inside the pair. A and C duplicate which; D mismatches the existing closing dash. After removing the interruption: The restored mural now occupies the gallery.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2471s)

19. **D — Single dash introducing an explanation**
The inspection revealed a single cause of the leak is complete. A single dash can introduce the explanatory noun phrase naming that cause. A semicolon cannot introduce this fragment; comma plus and also lacks an independent clause afterward.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2500s)

20. **A — Restrictive relative clause**
The restrictive that-clause identifies which samples retained their color. It belongs directly with samples. Adding punctuation would interrupt the noun and its identifying modifier or create a fragment.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1939s)

21. **C — Nonessential name after a unique role**
The first sentence identifies a unique conservation specialist. Mira Chen adds a nonessential name, so set it off with two commas: Its conservation specialist, Mira Chen, examined the manuscript. A comma only before or only after the name leaves the appositive unmatched; a semicolon splits the subject from its verb.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2841s)

22. **D — Integrated profession and name**
Marine biologist Elena Ruiz is one integrated title–name phrase that identifies whom the documentary follows. Do not separate the title from the identifying name. There is also no reason to place a comma before as she surveys here.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2841s)

23. **B — Work title with a following modifier**
Entitled directly introduces the name of the novel: the novel entitled The Cedar House. Do not place a comma or colon between entitled and the title it requires. The existing comma after the blank introduces the supplemental which-clause. A adds an invalid semicolon-plus-comma combination.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=3102s)

24. **A — Title–name shortcut exception: explanatory list**
The exhibition highlights two novels is a complete clause. The two titles then identify those novels, so a colon introduces the explanation. A period or semicolon would leave a noun-phrase fragment; comma plus which would begin a relative clause with no predicate.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2940s)

25. **C — Complex-list item boundaries**
Each item is a place name plus a comma and description. The first item ends after coastal village. A semicolon separates it from Bellford, matching the existing semicolon before the final item.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1029s)

26. **D — Comma inside a semicolon-list item**
The blank is inside the second item, between a person’s name and a nonessential description. Use a comma, matching the first and third items. The semicolon belongs after engineer, where the entire second item ends.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1142s)

27. **A — Colon before a complex list**
The project assigned three specialist teams is a complete clause, and the following list names those teams. A colon introduces that explanation. A period or semicolon would strand a noun-phrase list as a sentence fragment; comma plus which would begin a relative clause without a predicate. The later semicolons separate whole list items that contain internal commas.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=1179s)

28. **C — Indirect question**
This sentence reports an indirect question: The curator asked how... . It is a declarative sentence, so it ends with a period. The question word how inside it does not require a question mark.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=3326s)

29. **B — Direct question following a colon**
A direct question follows the colon. Its word order is how would the device respond, with the auxiliary would before the subject. End that direct question with a question mark.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=3424s)

30. **D — Conjunctive adverb between clauses**
The clauses are The original design required a separate sensor and the revised design combined both functions in one component. However is a conjunctive adverb, not a coordinating conjunction. A semicolon separates the clauses; the existing comma follows however.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=3480s)

31. **B — Transition placement: contrast with the preceding statement**
Later tests contradict the initial report, so however belongs to the middle clause: Later tests showed ... pressure, however. The final clause supplies supporting evidence rather than a contrast. A semicolon separates the middle and final independent clauses. C is a comma splice; D is a run-on.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=3878s)

32. **C — Transition placement: cause and effect**
Withstanding the test caused the approval. Therefore belongs at the beginning of the final clause, after the semicolon, followed by a comma. A puts therefore in the test-result clause instead; B and D fail to separate the independent clauses.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=3964s)

33. **D — Though as a parenthetical adverb**
Though means however here and interrupts the second clause, so set it off with commas: later measurements, though, revealed a flaw. The main clause remains later measurements revealed a flaw. A and B put a semicolon between the subject and its predicate; C omits the comma needed before the parenthetical though. The existing semicolon after stable already separates the two complete clauses.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=3650s)

34. **A — Matched parentheses**
The parenthetical noun phrase starts before a hand-carved flute and must close after flute. The main clause is The oldest instrument in the collection dates from the eighteenth century. Keep the opening and closing punctuation matched.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=2364s)

35. **B — Tracking different comma functions**
The comma between rough and uneven separates coordinate adjectives. The comma before which opens a supplemental modifier; the blank closes that modifier. The main clause is The sculpture’s rough, uneven surface provides evidence. Do not confuse the adjective comma with the modifier pair.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=5685s)

36. **D — Mixed punctuation and parallel list structure**
Track whole list items: title plus description. The second item ends after photographs, and the third starts at “River Voices.” A semicolon there matches the one after drawings. The commas inside each item serve a different purpose; the later period ends the completed list sentence.
[Review the video segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL61t23IOyE&t=5581s)
